Why Early European DVDs Used MP2 Audio

Early European DVD-Video releases frequently included MPEG-1 Audio Layer II (MP2) in their VOB files primarily due to regional hardware specifications, lower licensing costs, and alignment with existing European digital broadcasting standards. When the DVD standard was originally ratified, the DVD Forum mandated MP2 as the primary compressed audio standard for PAL territories, making it a guaranteed, hardware-supported audio format for early European consumers.

The Original PAL DVD Specification

When the DVD-Video format launched in the late 1990s, the DVD Forum established different baseline audio requirements for different television systems. For NTSC regions (such as North America and Japan), discs were required to include at least one audio track encoded in either Linear PCM (uncompressed) or Dolby Digital (AC-3).

For PAL regions, which encompassed Europe, the initial 1.0 specification mandated Linear PCM or MPEG-1 Audio Layer II (MP2). Under these original rules, Dolby Digital was merely an optional format. This meant that the earliest PAL DVD players sold in Europe were legally required to decode MP2 and PCM, but not necessarily AC-3. If a studio released a DVD in Europe with only an AC-3 track, first-generation players without AC-3 decoders could not play any sound. Mastering studios included MP2 tracks to guarantee universal compatibility across all European hardware.

Influence of European Electronics and the DVB Standard

The inclusion of MP2 was heavily championed by European technology companies, notably Philips, who had contributed significantly to the development of the MPEG audio formats. Europe was already standardizing on Digital Video Broadcasting (DVB) for digital terrestrial, satellite, and cable television, which utilized MP2 for its stereo audio layer. Integrating MP2 into the PAL DVD specification created synergy with European broadcast equipment, existing encoding pipelines, and consumer electronics components already in production.

Licensing Costs and Decoder Economics

Dolby Digital carried per-unit patent licensing fees for hardware manufacturers. In the late 1990s, omitting an AC-3 hardware decoder allowed European manufacturers to produce cheaper, entry-level DVD players. Including an MP2 audio track in the VOB container allowed disc publishers to target budget-conscious consumers who owned basic stereo players connected directly to standard CRT televisions via SCART cables, where multi-channel surround sound was unnecessary.

The Shift to Dolby Digital

Hollywood studios strongly opposed maintaining separate audio standards between regions, as they overwhelmingly preferred Dolby Digital 5.1 for their film catalogs. In December 1997, the DVD Forum amended the PAL specification to allow Dolby Digital as a standalone mandatory option, putting it on equal footing with MP2 and PCM.

Despite the specification change, mastering practices take time to adjust. Many European distributors continued to author DVD VOB files with MP2 stereo tracks through the early 2000s to support consumers who still owned first-generation PAL DVD players, as well as to minimize audio authoring costs for local-language releases, television series, and catalog titles.
