How VOB Pan and Scan Metadata Works on 4:3 TVs

Pan-and-scan metadata embedded within a DVD-Video VOB (MPEG-2) container allows a 16:9 widescreen video to dynamically adapt to a standard 4:3 television display. Instead of showing horizontal black bars (letterboxing) or simply stretching the image vertically, a DVD player uses this embedded positional data to dynamically extract and crop a 4:3 sub-frame from the widescreen source. This process tracks the primary action across the screen frame-by-frame or scene-by-scene, ensuring the 4:3 television screen is fully filled while keeping critical visual elements centered and in view.

The Storage of Pan-and-Scan Metadata in VOB Files

VOB files store video as MPEG-2 elementary streams. In an anamorphic widescreen DVD, the master video is stored at a standard resolution (720x480 for NTSC or 720x576 for PAL) with an aspect ratio flag signaling a 16:9 display.

Alongside the encoded frames, MPEG-2 allows for Sequence Display Extensions and Picture Display Extensions. These extensions store pan-and-scan metadata, specifically:

  • Display Dimensions: Defining the targeted window dimensions (typically an extraction window equivalent to a 4:3 area).
  • Frame Center Offsets: Horizontal offset vectors (and occasionally vertical offsets) that indicate the exact horizontal coordinate where the virtual 4:3 "camera" should be centered on the 16:9 frame.

How the Hardware Interprets the Data

For pan-and-scan metadata to alter playback, the DVD player must be configured in its system settings to output to a "4:3 Pan & Scan" display. The hardware processing follows a precise pipeline:

  1. Header Identification: When the player decodes a VOB file, it reads the aspect ratio information from the sequence header (16:9) and compares it with the user's hardware configuration (4:3).
  2. Offset Reading: In each video packet containing Picture Display Extensions, the player reads the frame center offset value.
  3. Cropping and Scaling: Using this coordinate, the player's internal video processor extracts a 540x480 portion (in NTSC) out of the original 720x480 matrix. The offset value determines whether this crop occurs on the far left, dead center, far right, or anywhere dynamically in between.
  4. Signal Output: The cropped sub-frame is horizontally scaled to the standard 720-pixel width of the analog composite, S-Video, or component video signal fed to the television.

The Result on a 4:3 Screen

On a traditional 4:3 CRT television, this process creates the illusion that the film was natively shot or professionally reframed for a square screen. Because the pan-and-scan metadata can vary continuously, the display dynamically "pans" across wide vistas to follow dialogue or fast-paced motion. Viewers receive a full-screen image with preserved vertical resolution, driven completely in real-time by the metadata instructions rather than relying on a permanently altered, hard-cropped secondary video track on the disc.
